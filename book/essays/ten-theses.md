# Ten theses for the book

*2026-11-14, night. Ten ways to say what the book is about, for a night's sleep. Most repackage things we said today; numbers 4, 7 and 9 are curveballs. None is a recommendation.*

## The ten

1. **Agents build; Shale reshapes.** An agent writes a working app in an afternoon without any help from Shale. Shale earns its place when the work turns to reshaping it, gating it and keeping several repos consistent with each other, which is most of a codebase's life.

2. **Cleaning up a legacy web of apps, safely, with a programmer's judgment.** Today's Cobblestone removal is the template: survey, prove nothing is lost, fix the dependents, delete, gate. A company with two hundred repos does the same steps, and the agent does them while you decide.

3. **Strictness over convenience, with the convenience moved into the stop.** A language an agent writes should refuse more than a human's would, and say more when it refuses. The stop that names the fix is where the language's kindness lives.

4. **Curveball: the agent's transcript is the design document.** Shale's best feature requests today came from counting its own agent's launches: a quarter of heredocs going around it for `grep`, 228 child runs for one verdict. A book could follow that loop: watch what the agent does, count it, and let the census decide what the language grows.

5. **Refactoring after the death of sloppy code.** Today's agents rarely write mess to clean up. What remains is reshaping for new requirements and proving the reshape changed nothing. Shale is a toolkit for that kind of refactoring: rename and prove, move and judge, a census and then a gate.

6. **A language that grows the words real work asks for, the day it asks.** Tables for the essay surface in an hour, `grep_tree` the afternoon the census found the gap. The book is the story of a language growing, chapter by chapter, out of what one project needed that day.

7. **Curveball: the book is a decommissioning.** Follow one real migration from start to finish: bringing apps off webbox and back to plain Linux, which is tomorrow's work. Every chapter is a step of taking something apart without breaking what stands on it, and Shale is the crowbar with a safety catch.

8. **The human decides; the agent does everything else, checkably.** The paradox of the human's job, steering without doing, made concrete. Shale's job is to make the agent's work checkable at a glance, so the human can steer at the agent's speed.

9. **Curveball: BusyBox in reverse.** BusyBox kept Unix's commands and cut their options so old scripts would run small. Shale kept the jobs and cut the commands so new scripts would run safe. A book that explains a tool by what it chose not to keep, one refusal per chapter: no pipes, no `sed`, no closures, no aliases.

10. **Your developer box is a codebase.** Its repos, worktrees, build folders, dotfiles and memory are one system, and keeping it clean is refactoring, not system administration. Shale lets you wear a programmer's hat for all of it, and the agent brings the sysadmin's caution.

## A note on the curveballs

Number 4 makes the agent's own behavior the subject. It's the most novel of the ten and the riskiest, because it may be a chapter rather than a book. Number 7 gives the book a plot with a beginning and an end, which the essay-surface spine never had. Number 9 is a way of teaching, not a thesis: it could carry any of the others.
