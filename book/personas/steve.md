# How Steve prompts

Drawn from 144 console messages (2026-11-12 to 11-14) and 17 essay-surface replies. It guides drafted prompts under MISSION PI.

## Length and shape

Short. The median message is about 150 characters, or 25 words. A third are under 50 characters ("yes", "deployed", "keep going", "replies in"), and only about one in eight runs past 500. The longest is about 200 words. He writes plain paragraphs, never bulleted lists; the one enumeration was inline ("1) rename some chapters of 2) add subtitles").

A working prompt usually opens with "Let's", or with a judgment ("The refusal chapter is quite long."). It often ends with a next step or a delivery instruction rather than a summary: "Save a cold agent lane for this inventory," "Show me the cold agent's findings on the essay surface," "Let's compact first." Typos and the odd "haha" stay in.

## What he specifies and what he leaves open

He specifies the goal, the reason, and the standard to judge by. He often fixes a count or cap ("eight chapters max", "900 words"). On wording he's exact when a word bothers him, and otherwise says "let the agent propose names." He leaves method, layout and sequencing to the agent, and checks premises first: "let's make sure my premise is correct."

## The kinds of prompts

Rough shares of the 144:
- **Approval, status and go-ahead,** about 40%: "lgtm", "better, ship it".
- **Corrections and critiques** of prose or design, about 20%.
- **Standards and rules** for the book or the agents, about 12%.
- **Broad plans and missions,** about 10%, usually the longest messages, at the start of a phase.
- **Delegation and orchestration** (cold agents, lanes, autonomous runs), about 10%.
- **Questions,** about 8%.

## How he corrects

Directly, without heat, usually with the reason. He quotes the sentence and names the fault: "Books don't read. Books show or teach." He owns his own errors: "I said 'let it breathe' but see now that it just doesn't work, so that's my bad." Praise is frequent and specific. A strong rule shows up as repetition, an instruction to enforce it with a gate, or a rare edge: "We have used this flow so many times; it's unclear why you are confused about it." He invites pushback: "Push back on me if that's inaccurate."

## On the essay surface

Even shorter, and pinned to one paragraph: "fine", "do it", "100%.", "add a defensive check". The quote supplies the context, so a reply is a verdict or a one-line amendment. Longer replies argue a position. The console plans and steers; the essay surface decides.

## Habits worth imitating, and ones a reader may not share

Worth imitating: a reason with every rule; a fixed count; a premise check before rework; naming where output goes; correcting from a quoted example.

Possibly not shared: his terseness rests on a mission document, memories and long shared history. "keep going" works because the agent knows the lane; a reader starting cold needs more.

## Where the book's drafted prompts differ

The drafts run 160 to 260 words, the top of his range, polished and self-contained, ending with a full report spec.
- **Fine for a book:** the length and self-containment, since the reader lacks the context; clean grammar; the stated reasons, which are true to him.
- **Misleading:** a complete prompt suggests one message carries a session. Really, work moves through many short follow-ups, and the book should show some. Targets like "a few milliseconds" overstate his focus on speed, which he said he'd "never be quite so obsessed with" in a first version. Lines in which "I" drafts notes are wrong, since the agent writes the notes and he replies. Literary turns ("make me believe it") are more polished than he writes.
